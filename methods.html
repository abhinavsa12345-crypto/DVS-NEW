Section 03

Methods & Derivations

Multi-Attribute Utility Theory, sales-response optimisation, makespan scheduling and concentration analysis — each derived from first principles.

Four techniques, each derived from first principles

The project's central claim is that none of its formulas are invented for convenience. Each rests on established theory, and each derivation is reproduced in full in the accompanying report.

Technique 1

Multi-Attribute Utility Theory

Why the five scores are added rather than multiplied — the question any examiner asks first.

1

The problem class

We must rank alternatives (doctors) differing on several criteria at once. This is the standard multi-criteria decision problem addressed by MAUT, formalised by Keeney & Raiffa (1976).

2

The condition that must hold

Mutual preferential independence. The trade-off accepted between any two attributes must not depend on the levels of the others. If every subset of attributes is preferentially independent of its complement, an additive value function exists — Theorem 3.6.

3

Why the sub-functions vanish

The general form is V(x) = Σ wᵢ vᵢ(xᵢ). Here every pillar is already scored on a shared anchored 0–10 scale, so vᵢ is the identity map and V collapses to Σ wᵢ sᵢ — the practical simplification Edwards (1977) formalised as SMART.

4

Proof of bounds

Because Σwᵢ = 1 with all weights positive, DVS is a convex combination and lies within the range of its inputs. All pillars at 10 gives 10 × 1 = 10.00. At the rubric floors (2,2,2,1,1) the minimum is 1.75.

DVS_max = 10 × Σ wᵢ = 10 × 1 = 10.00 DVS_min = 2(0.30) + 2(0.25) + 2(0.20) + 1(0.15) + 1(0.10) = 1.75
Technique 2

Sales-response optimisation

The strongest analytical result in the project: why higher-value doctors should receive more visits is a derived conclusion, not a management preference.

1

Response to detailing is concave

Empirical work consistently finds diminishing returns — the second call in a month produces less incremental prescribing than the first. This underpins Lodish's CALLPLAN (1971) and is confirmed econometrically by Manchanda, Rossi & Chintagunta (2004).

2

Formalising the allocation problem

Let gd(f) be the response of doctor d to f calls per month, with g′ > 0 and g″ < 0. Maximise total response subject to the capacity constraint, then form the Lagrangian and take first-order conditions.

maximise Σ_d g_d(f_d) subject to Σ_d f_d ≤ C ℒ = Σ g_d(f_d) − λ( Σ f_d − C ) ∂ℒ/∂f_d = 0 ⟹ g′_d(f_d) = λ for every funded doctor
3

What the condition means

At the optimum, the marginal return of the last call is equal across all doctors — not the total return. Doctors with higher response elasticity therefore receive more calls, until their marginal return falls to the common shadow price λ.

4

Why this implies a tier ladder

A four-tier frequency ladder is a step-function approximation of that continuous optimum. The strategy is thus a defensible discretisation of a well-established optimisation result.

Technique 3

Makespan scheduling for territory design

Territories must balance workload, not headcount — a territory heavy in Platinum doctors must carry fewer doctors overall.

minimise max_j W_j where W_j = Σ_{d ∈ territory j} f_tier(d)

This is multiprocessor scheduling — partitioning jobs (doctors, with processing time equal to visit frequency) across identical machines (representatives) to minimise the maximum load. The problem is NP-hard, so a heuristic is required.

The model uses Longest Processing Time first (LPT): sort doctors by workload descending, then assign each to the least-loaded territory. Graham (1969) proved a worst-case bound:

LPT makespan ≤ ( 4/3 − 1/(3m) ) × optimal with m = 6 territories → 1.278 × optimal, typically far better

Balance is reported as the coefficient of variation of workload across territories. Industry practice accepts CV ≤ 10%; the model achieves 1.8%.

Technique 4

Concentration analysis

The one calculation capable of invalidating the entire approach — which is precisely why it belongs in the analysis.

G = 1 − Σ_k (X_k − X_{k−1})(Y_k + Y_{k−1}) X = cumulative share of doctors · Y = cumulative share of total value

Rank all doctors by DVS ascending, then plot cumulative share of doctors against cumulative share of value. Perfect equality is the 45° line; the Lorenz curve bows below it. G is twice the area between them.

Why this is the critical diagnostic. G = 0 would mean every doctor is equally valuable — in which case differentiated targeting would be pointless and the entire strategy unjustified. Including this test, rather than quietly omitting it, is what separates a model from an argument.
