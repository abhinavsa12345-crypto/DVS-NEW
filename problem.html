Section 01

The Business Problem

Why uniform sales coverage fails — stated quantitatively, not rhetorically.

600 prescribers6 representatives ₹30 lakhs / quarter6-month window

Four constraints, operating at once

Each constraint alone would be manageable. Together they make uniform coverage arithmetically impossible rather than merely inefficient.

ConstraintRealityImplication
Field force6 medical representatives A scarce, expensive asset. Every call must be justified by expected return.
Doctor universe500–800 relevant prescribers Physically cannot all be covered. Selection is forced, not optional.
Promotional budget₹30 lakhs per quarter Every rupee on a low-value doctor is withheld from a high-value one.
Time6 months to establish Prescribing habits form early; a wait-and-see approach forfeits the window.

The arithmetic of failure

The case against uniform coverage does not rest on judgement. It follows directly from the numbers.

Coverage dilution

Available calls = 6 MRs × 22 working days × 9 calls/day = 1,188 per month 1,188 calls ÷ 600 doctors = ≈ 2 calls per doctor per month … but only if every call goes to a doctor — nothing for chemists, hospitals or admin. Reserving realistic non-doctor time → one visit every two months

Pharmaceutical detailing depends on repetition and relationship. A contact frequency of once per two months sits below the threshold at which brand recall and prescribing habit form. The coverage is nominal rather than effective.

Budget dilution

₹30,00,000 ÷ 600 doctors = ₹5,000 per doctor per quarter

At this level the allocation funds neither meaningful sampling, nor a CME programme, nor scientific engagement of any substance. It is spread too thinly to change behaviour anywhere.

Uniform coverage produces uniform ineffectiveness. Spending equally across 600 doctors does not distribute impact evenly — it eliminates impact entirely, because no doctor receives enough contact or investment to cross the threshold where behaviour changes.

Why single-axis targeting also fails

Conventional segmentation ranks doctors on one dimension — patient volume, usually as prescribing deciles. This is convenient and dangerously incomplete.

Doctor A — ranks high on volume

Sees 50 relevant patients a day. But is loyal to a competitor brand, sits on that competitor's advisory board, and has adopted no new molecule in four years.

Will not prescribe within the launch window.

Doctor B — ranks low on volume

Sees 20 relevant patients a day. But has adopted three new molecules in two years, teaches at a medical college, and has voiced dissatisfaction with existing therapy.

Likely early adopter with peer influence.

A volume-ranked model places Doctor A far above Doctor B. Every practical consideration says the opposite. A single-axis model cannot represent this; a multi-attribute model can.

The reframed question

Not “how do we reach all 600 doctors?” — a question whose honest answer is “we cannot” — but “which doctors will generate the majority of prescriptions, how do we identify them before the launch rather than after, and how do we prove the resulting plan is deliverable?”

What a solution must satisfy

  • Multi-dimensional — volume alone is demonstrably insufficient.
  • Feasible — the call plan must fit real field capacity, and this must be shown, not assumed.
  • Proportional — budget and visits must track expected value, not headcount.
  • Adaptive — the model must re-score doctors on observed behaviour at a defined interval.
  • Transparent — every number traceable to a formula, every formula to a justification.
