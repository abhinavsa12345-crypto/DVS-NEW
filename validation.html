The strategic architecture proved sound — segmentation logic, resource allocation and tiering are well constructed and internally coherent. The defects found are defects of arithmetic and presentation, not of reasoning. Correcting them strengthens the case, because it removes the objections a sceptical reader would otherwise raise.

The six material errors

1 · Breakeven overstated by five to six months

Claimed: Month 14–15. Actual: Month 19–21 on the document's own numbers.

Position at Month 6 : ₹30.04L revenue − ₹87.00L investment = −₹56.96L Months 7–12 recovery : ₹86.4L − ₹64.0L = +₹22.40L Cumulative at M12 : −₹34.56L Needed to clear by M15: ₹34.56L ÷ 3 months = ₹11.52L / month Actual H2 run-rate : ₹22.40L ÷ 6 months = ₹3.73L / month Required uplift : 3.1 × — nothing in the plan generates this

If monthly operating breakeven was meant rather than cumulative payback, that occurs around Month 11–12 — but that is a different and much weaker claim, and the two must never be conflated.

2 · “140% ROI” is a mislabelled ratio

₹2.10L revenue ÷ ₹1.50L cost = 140% is a revenue-to-cost ratio, not a return on investment. ROI = (gain − cost) / cost = 40%. Applying a 70% gross margin gives contribution of ₹1.47L against ₹1.50L cost — an ROI of approximately −2%. The breakeven gross margin for the programme is 71.4%.

3 · No downside case anywhere

Every figure sat at the optimistic end of its stated range — Month 6 modelled at 320 prescribers and 5,120 prescriptions, the maximum of the stated band — then compounded multiplicatively. Three scenarios (70% / base / 120%) are now modelled instead.

4 · Bronze budget contradicts its own policy band

The policy table specifies 5–8% of budget for Bronze; the applied allocation uses 12% — fifty percent above the ceiling. At 8%, Bronze would take ₹2.4L, releasing ₹1.2L to raise Platinum spend to roughly ₹23,000 per doctor.

5 · Stated minimum DVS is unattainable

The document states a minimum of 1.60. Re-derivation from its own published rubric floors (2, 2, 2, 1, 1) gives 1.75, and no admissible combination produces 1.60.

6 · Capacity rounded up, pessimistic case untested

22 × 9 = 198 was rounded to “approximately 200”. At the lower bound of 8 calls/day, regional capacity is 1,056 — 12% below the planning figure. The good news, now stated explicitly: the plan still passes at 76% utilisation.

The opinion-leader caveat

Pillar 3 rests on the Columbia tetracycline study (Coleman, Katz & Menzel, 1966), which found network-central doctors adopted earlier. But van den Bulte & Lilien (2001) re-analysed that same dataset with newly collected advertising data and found the contagion effect largely disappears once marketing effort is controlled for — the company had simply detailed influential doctors harder.

A textbook correlation-causation trap. This project therefore treats opinion-leader influence as real but bounded, uses econometrically identified estimates rather than inflated industry multipliers, and recommends measuring it with a matched-control design — comparing doctors inside a KOL's network against comparable doctors outside it, holding promotional intensity constant.

Additional known limitations

  • Additive models cannot express conjunctive logic. A doctor scoring 1 on accessibility still scores respectably if other pillars are high, yet is operationally unreachable. Such doctors are flagged for a channel change rather than penalised in the score.
  • Linearity is imposed by the anchors. Equal-interval rubrics assume a linear value relationship; prescription potential in particular is likely concave.
  • Geography is not modelled in territory balancing. Production deployment must layer geographic clustering on top, which will degrade balance somewhat.
  • Adoption is modelled as linear rather than following a Bass S-curve, which front-loads revenue and flatters the early months.
